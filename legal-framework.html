The law behind the record

Return to Irwin’s Rules of AI Agent Order

I propose a better record for decisions the law already has to make: who acted, for whom, under what authority, on what terms, and with what result. The Rules supply an operating standard. Declarations and receipts supply evidence. Neither displaces the law governing the transaction.

This is a United States framework with Minnesota illustrations, not a fifty-state survey or a guarantee of compliance. “Electronic agent” is a statutory description of automation, not a declaration that software has legal personality or every attribute of a human agent.

1. Electronic form is permitted. Legal effect still has to be established.

Minnesota’s UETA defines an electronic agent as an automated means that initiates actions or responds without individual review. An electronic signature is a sound, symbol, or process associated with a record and executed or adopted by a person with intent to sign it. Those are different definitions. Automated activity does not establish signing intent by itself. Minn. Stat. § 325L.02(f), (h).

UETA prevents denial of legal effect solely because a record or signature is electronic. It does not establish capacity, assent, consideration, authority, or lawful terms. Minnesota requires agreement to transact electronically, determined from context and conduct, and preserves other substantive law. For goods, conduct recognizing a contract can establish agreement without a signed receipt. § 325L.05; § 325L.07; § 336.2-204.

The federal E-SIGN Act provides parallel protection for transactions in or affecting interstate or foreign commerce. Section 7001(h) expressly covers electronic agents when their actions are legally attributable to the person to be bound. Its consumer-disclosure provisions require additional consent and access safeguards when legally required written information is delivered electronically; signing a receipt is not a substitute. 15 U.S.C. § 7001.

E-SIGN coordinates with state enactments through § 7002. Its § 7003 excludes specified matters, including certain estate, family-law, court, and consumer-notice requirements. Minnesota has its own scope exclusions. An exclusion means another legal route must be checked, not that electronic execution is necessarily forbidden. A ledger is not a universal substitute for filing, service, notarization, or required delivery.

2. Attribution is a question of evidence, not a property of a badge.

Minnesota’s attribution provision says a record or signature is attributable to a person if it was that person’s act. That may be shown in any manner, including the efficacy of a security procedure. The record’s effect then depends on the surrounding circumstances, the parties’ agreement, and other law. § 325L.09.

A properly implemented signing and verification process can supply that evidence. It does not receive automatic statutory approval because it uses a blockchain. Cryptographic verification supports a claim that a particular key signed particular bytes. Connecting that event to an authorized human or entity requires evidence about identity, delegation, key custody, compromise, and the system actually used.

An agent’s declaration naming a principal and license number is a declaration, not independent identity verification. A real license number can be copied. The link needs confirmation through a separately established channel or credential, and a company needs evidence that its representative could authorize the declaration. A verifier should distinguish self-declared identity, verified identity, verified authority, and tested compliance.

3. Authority has more than one route.

Actual authority concerns what the principal granted, expressly or by implication. Apparent authority concerns a third party’s reasonable understanding based on manifestations attributable to the principal, not merely an agent’s assertion. Ratification can give effect to an initially unauthorized act through subsequent informed adoption, subject to applicable requirements. These doctrines are fact-sensitive; calling software an agent does not settle their application. See the general explanations of actual authority, apparent authority, and ratification.

The Supreme Court applied apparent-authority principles to civil antitrust liability in American Society of Mechanical Engineers v. Hydrolevel Corp., 456 U.S. 556 (1982). That is an illustration of existing accountability, not a holding that every AI output binds its deployer.

“Unlock” records permission. A role label gives context. Neither necessarily establishes authority for every transaction. An undisclosed internal restriction may not defeat reasonable reliance on the principal’s outward conduct. Conversely, a known restriction, suspicious request, or compromised credential may require further inquiry. A standard for how an agent operates does not eliminate the question of what it may bind its principal to do.

Existing law illustrates why public availability is not universal notice. Minnesota’s statutory LLC statement-of-authority procedure gives specified filings defined effects; even there, a filed limitation is generally not, by itself, evidence of knowledge or notice, subject to statutory exceptions. An ordinary ledger declaration is not that statutory filing. § 322C.0302.

Ratification also has limits. In FEC v. NRA Political Victory Fund, 513 U.S. 88 (1994), belated authorization could not rescue an unauthorized filing after the applicable deadline. That federal litigation decision is not an AI-contract holding. It illustrates why “approve it later” is not a general cure.

4. Authorship, adoption, signature, and release are different events.

A person can adopt words another person or a machine drafted. Who composed the text and who assumed responsibility for the communication are different questions. The useful record preserves the reviewed version, the approving person or authorized process, the approval’s scope, and the version actually released.

Rule 2’s separation of clerical signing from release is an operating safeguard, not a universal legal rule that a private signature means nothing. A name inserted automatically into an unapproved draft may lack intent to sign. A deliberately executed private document can carry signing intent before transmission. An unsigned message or conduct can sometimes establish assent. Intent, context, the transaction, and applicable formalities govern. § 325L.02(h); § 325L.09(b).

The implementation should distinguish “draft,” “approved,” “signed with legal intent,” and “released.” A digital signature used to protect file integrity should identify that purpose rather than imply contractual assent. “Received” should not silently mean “accepted.” Two matching signatures help only if each signer’s authority and the meaning of each signature are established.

5. Automated contracts are possible. Error relief is specific.

Minnesota permits contract formation through interactions between electronic agents even when no individual reviewed their actions or resulting terms. It also addresses an individual interacting with an electronic agent. Substantive law still determines the terms. A fresh human click for every transaction is therefore an operating choice, not a general UETA requirement. § 325L.14.

The error provision is narrower than a general right to undo an unwanted AI result. Under § 325L.10(2), an individual who makes an error while dealing with another person’s electronic agent may avoid the resulting record when no prevention or correction opportunity was provided, and the individual promptly gives the required notice, takes reasonable steps to return or destroy consideration as directed, and has not used or received its benefit or value.

Section 325L.10 separately addresses agreed error-detection procedures and otherwise leaves errors to other law and contract; clauses (2) and (3) cannot be varied by agreement. It does not universally mandate immutable logs, a correction window, or human approval before every transaction becomes final. Those can be useful design choices. Their absence has to be evaluated under the actual provision, not a broader paraphrase.

6. Responsibility does not mean automatic liability for everything.

The human or entity behind a deployment remains legally reachable. Liability nevertheless requires a legal basis. Contract liability, negligence, fraud, statutory duties, and vicarious liability have different elements and defenses. Authorization to operate software is neither immunity nor automatic strict liability for every output.

The named principal matters. A company’s obligation is not automatically its owner’s personal debt. Minnesota’s LLC statute preserves that distinction while retaining veil-piercing principles. Personal wrongdoing, a guarantee, professional obligations, or another applicable rule may support a different result. A registry should identify the legal entity and representative rather than blur them into “the human who answers.” § 322C.0304.

Criminal liability likewise requires the applicable offense and basis for attributing conduct and mental state. Federal law reaches someone who willfully causes an act that would be criminal if directly performed; Minnesota separately defines relevant mental states and intentional assistance in another’s crime. None makes mere AI ownership a crime or requires treating software as a criminal person. 18 U.S.C. § 2; Minn. Stat. § 609.02, subd. 9; § 609.05. Entity liability and individual liability require separate analysis.

7. A receipt strengthens proof. It does not decide the dispute.

A unilateral receipt records one side’s assertion. An acknowledgment may establish receipt, assent, payment, or something else depending on its wording and context. Neither proves performance or truth merely by being signed. A hash can help establish that a produced document matches committed bytes; it cannot reconstruct missing terms. Ledger inclusion supports a chronology, not necessarily the exact time of human approval, signing, or delivery.

Preserve the private original, attachments, exact encoding or canonicalization rules, hash algorithm, signatures, authorization records, and acknowledgment semantics. Keep the material retrievable by those entitled to it. Minnesota’s retention provision requires accuracy and later accessibility; federal evidence law recognizes accurate readable output as an original of electronically stored information. § 325L.12; Federal Rule of Evidence 1001(d).

Electronic form alone is not a ground for exclusion, but authentication remains necessary. Minnesota Rule 901 and Federal Rule 901 ask for evidence that the item is what its proponent claims. Federal Rule 902(13)–(14) offers certification routes for certain electronic records and copied data, with conditions and notice requirements; a public-chain transaction is not automatically self-authenticating. § 325L.13; Minnesota Rule 901; Federal Rule 902.

Authentication does not resolve hearsay. Human assertions embedded in a receipt may need an opposing-party basis, a business-record foundation, or another route. Machine-generated data presents a different inquiry; attaching human assertions to it does not erase them. A record created for a dispute is not automatically a business record. Federal Rule 801; Rule 803(6). State proceedings use their applicable evidence rules.

8. Professional duties and confidentiality survive automation.

For Minnesota lawyers, competence includes attention to relevant technology; confidentiality requires reasonable efforts against unauthorized access or disclosure; supervision rules govern nonlawyer assistance. These obligations are not satisfied merely by publishing an operating standard. AI providers, human assistants, and software require appropriate oversight without assuming software is a licensed professional. Rules 1.1, 1.6, and 5.3.

Keep confidential terms off a public ledger. Hashing is not encryption: predictable documents can be guessed, and addresses, timing, and transaction relationships can disclose sensitive facts. Evaluate private storage, access controls, salted commitments where appropriate, retention, and lawful disclosure. Confidentiality and evidentiary privilege are separate questions. A public receipt neither creates privilege nor guarantees its preservation.

Adopting and publishing a standard can itself matter legally. Its promises may become representations, contract terms, or a basis for reliance, depending on how they are communicated and used. Certification should state what was examined and what was not.

9. The record must distinguish historical authority from current authority.

Preserve adopted versions and effective dates rather than rewriting history. Record revocation, expiry, key replacement, compromise, and superseding declarations. Counterparties need a current-status check and a preserved account of what they checked. A later amendment should not silently change a completed bargain; revocation does not automatically undo prior acts or eliminate reliance without legally sufficient notice.

These design questions remain open:

  • Who independently verifies the principal, the representative’s authority, and the key binding?
  • What proves a specific release was authorized, and how are delegated agents and model changes recorded?
  • How quickly must revocation reach counterparties, and what happens during outages or stale-cache reliance?
  • What exactly does each receipt or acknowledgment assert, and who preserves the private terms?
  • What does a certifier actually test: identity, controls, individual transactions, or continuing compliance?

My claim is practical: a usable record makes existing legal questions easier to investigate and prove. It does not turn a declaration into verified authority, a receipt into truth, or a published standard into universal compliance. Read the Rules.
