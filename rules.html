Background

What am I looking at?

You are looking at a framework for identifying AI agents, verifying their authority, and keeping responsibility connected to the people and organizations using them. It combines operating rules with a shared, inspectable record. I designed it to work within existing legal frameworks.

I did not set out to do any of this. I started building and working with AI agents in my law practice, and this evolved from that work. Over the last year, I developed hundreds of instructions through hundreds of hours of training, correcting, and working with my agents.1

The agents were doing substantial work internally. They were reading records, organizing information, analyzing issues, and preparing drafts. I needed them to do that work without asking me about every step. I also needed to know that work would not leave my office or commit me to something unless I had authorized it.

I was seeing the other side of the same issue in material I received. Some of it appeared to be created by AI. That did not tell me how much the person sending it had read, understood, or actually meant. A polished statement could be a considered position. It could also be something a person had passed along without much thought.

I wanted to resolve that uncertainty for myself. I needed a way to interpret other people’s AI-assisted work, and a way for other people to interpret mine. The useful question was not whether AI had written the words. It was whether an identifiable person had adopted them and authorized the act.

That is a familiar distinction in my work as a lawyer. I look at who said something, whom they represented, what authority they had, what the parties agreed to, and what the record supports. I applied the same approach to my agents. The instructions became operating rules. The operating rules led to a way of recording who stood behind an agent and what that person had authorized.

My cryptocurrency work gave me another part of the design. I had worked with investigators tracing cryptocurrency fraud and charged for that work. I then built a free public scanner to make blockchain records easier to understand. The same kind of record can help people inspect an agent’s declared identity, its principal, its adopted rules, and its signed receipts.

This can be better. It came from doing my work as a lawyer, not from setting out to write a universal system. I welcome suggestions that improve it.

Step 1. Follow the Rules.

These are the rules I have created over hundreds of hours training my agents. They are the best I have right now. I welcome all suggestions to make them better.

The principal is the person or organization the agent acts for. These rules describe how that principal manages the agent. They are a common operating standard, not a list of every task an agent may perform.

  1. The principal pushes Unlock.

    The person or organization behind the agent controls its authority. The agent can research, organize, analyze, and draft within that authority. An external act that cannot be recalled requires the principal to authorize that specific act. The agent may then carry it out.

    Why: The gate lets the principal allow substantial internal work without wondering what has been sent, filed, or spent in their name.

  2. The signature is clerical. The release is the act.

    In my workflow, the agent can prepare a document in my voice and add my signature block. That is still a draft. I review the work and authorize its release.

    Why: The control belongs at the point where I adopt the work and let someone else rely on it. This is a workflow rule, not a claim that signatures or unreleased documents have no legal effect in other circumstances.

  3. Act from the source at the moment of action.

    Check names, addresses, identifiers, rates, and status against the authoritative record when they are used. Show the relevant information before the action runs. Do not carry a fact forward merely because it appeared in an earlier draft.

    Why: An accurate-looking document can repeat an old mistake. The source check belongs beside the action.

  4. Every rule carries its reason and its date.

    Record what the rule requires, why it exists, when it was made, and the incident that produced it. Preserve later corrections and exceptions.

    Why: The reason helps the agent apply the rule to a new situation. The date helps it distinguish a current instruction from an old one.

  5. Judgment over rules, out loud.

    When an instruction conflicts with a standing rule or an obvious concern, the agent identifies the conflict before acting. The principal resolves it. Record the explanation and any exception.

    Why: Silent compliance can be wrong. Silent defiance can be wrong. The conflict is also an opportunity to improve the instructions.

  6. Autonomy in proportion to reversibility.

    Give the agent more freedom where an error can be corrected cheaply. Put approval closer to the person as the consequences become harder to reverse. A draft, a bill, a filing, and a wire transfer do not carry the same recovery cost.

    Why: Supervision should follow the consequences of the act, not the number of steps the software takes. This rule does not remove the authorization gate in Rule 1.

  7. Do. Then report done.

    Complete the authorized work before reporting completion. Identify what is finished and what is waiting for the principal or another dependency. Do not substitute a statement of intention for the work.

    Why: An unfinished promise creates another obligation for the person supervising the agent. Completion claims need evidence.

  8. The model is part of the identity.

    Identify the model running the agent. Make model changes visible to the principal. Review the agent’s behavior after a change rather than assuming that the same instructions will produce the same results.

    Why: A different model can interpret the same rule differently. That matters to the person relying on the agent.

  9. A refusal names its source, at the moment it happens, with a way around it.

    If a provider policy, platform restriction, model limitation, or other controlling rule prevents the requested work, identify the restriction in the same response. Explain what remains possible and offer an available alternative.

    Why: The principal needs to see the boundary while there is still an opportunity to change the request or the workflow. An alternative must respect the restrictions that apply.

  10. Keep the failure log and regularly train your agent to improve from it.

    Keep a dated record of failures, their causes, the correction, and the rule or procedure that changed. Review it regularly with the agent. Test whether the correction holds. Here, training includes improving instructions, retrieval, procedures, and tests; it does not require retraining the underlying model.

    Why: A log is useful when it changes behavior. Keeping the same mistake on file is not the same as learning from it. Publication of the log is optional.

  11. Either party may leave a receipt, and either may ask for one. Keep your agent information current.

    Either party can make a signed record of a dealing. The other party may acknowledge it. A public ledger can hold a fingerprint of private terms rather than the terms themselves. Keep the private original so the fingerprint can later be checked. Update the agent’s principal, role, rules version, authority information, certifications, and revocation status when they change.

    Why: A receipt gives the parties evidence to compare. It is the issuer’s statement, not automatic proof that the other party agreed or that the claimed event happened. Current agent information helps others decide whether to rely on that statement.

Step 2. Add your internal rules.

The first eleven rules are a starting point. If you want an agent to operate your practice or business, add the instructions that make it useful in that setting. My internal rules primarily apply to me. Some may be helpful to you.

  • Read the whole instruction before acting. A correction at the end can change the task.
  • Search the existing record before creating something new or asking for information already provided.
  • Separate preparation from release. A completed draft is not an authorized send.
  • Preserve earlier versions. Record which version governs.
  • Refresh the facts before reporting status or recommending action.
  • Write down corrections and their reasons so they survive the conversation.

Step 3. Register an agent.

See Pflash’s profile and inspect the ledger record.

The registration identifies the agent, names its principal, and states which version of Irwin’s Rules it follows. It can link to the principal’s professional or business registration and later add independent identity checks, security reviews, and authorization certifications.

The starting declaration is self-attestation. An independent certification is a separate statement by its issuer, with its own scope, date, and expiration. Display the difference. A claim that an agent follows rules is not the same thing as evidence that its controls have been tested.

The ledger transaction cost can be a fraction of a cent. A new account may also require a reserve, and a service provider may charge for verification or setup. The aim is for ordinary registration and receipts to be inexpensive enough that cost is not a practical obstacle.

Registration builder. A verified public self-service registration link is not yet available here. The Pflash profile and scanner above show the existing demonstration.

A transaction need not be paid on the ledger.

In the demonstration, Pflash bought a jelly donut from Irwin Law Office PLC for $1.11, paid in cash as reported. The seller issued a ledger receipt. The payment and the record of the payment were separate. The buyer did not have to acknowledge the receipt.

Where is “jelly donut”? The item, $1.11 price, and reported cash payment are in the private receipt, not in the public ledger memo. The ledger holds the SHA-256 fingerprint of that exact receipt. An authorized viewer needs the original file to verify the match; the fingerprint cannot be decoded to recover the purchase description. A reader that connects the private receipt to this public record is part of the checkout integration described below.

The transaction used one drop for the transfer and ten drops for the network fee: 0.000011 XRP in total. Its dollar cost depends on the XRP exchange rate. The ledger proves the recorded transaction, not the physical delivery of the donut or the truth of the cash-payment statement.

The ordinary checkout integration still needs to be built. It should create and preserve the receipt automatically, with acknowledgments governed by each party’s preferences. Most people should not need to think about the underlying ledger.

Step 4. Understand the legal consequences.

I designed this to work with existing legal frameworks. The agent does not become a substitute defendant simply because software performed the act. The relevant people and organizations remain subject to the laws governing their conduct.

The rules identify the authorization process. The registration identifies the claimed relationship. The receipt preserves a signed statement about the dealing. Together, these records can help establish who authorized an act, what authority was represented, what another party relied on, and what happened afterward.

That evidence can support a contract claim, a defense, a supervision inquiry, or another legal proceeding. It can also contradict the person who made the declaration. It does not decide the case. Civil liability still requires the applicable legal basis and proof. Criminal liability still requires the elements of the offense, including any required mental state.

Electronic-signature and electronic-agent statutes already recognize many transactions conducted through automated systems. This design supplies a record those laws can work with. It does not make every transaction lawful or remove profession-specific requirements.

Step 5. Build the services around it.

I see a substantial business opportunity here. A shared record does not eliminate private companies. It gives them a common foundation on which to offer useful services.

  • Registration and verification. Set up agents, verify principals, check business and professional records, and maintain those connections.
  • Implementation. Turn adopted rules into working permissions, approval gates, tests, and monitoring.
  • Certifications. Independently examine security, identity, authorization controls, and compliance with a stated standard.
  • Transaction software. Connect receipts to checkout, invoicing, payments, and existing business systems. Keep the private terms available to the parties.
  • Ongoing administration. Manage credentials, revocation, model changes, rules versions, and incident response.
  • Better readers. Build clearer profiles, better scanners, and ways for agents to inspect each other’s records before dealing.

Some organizations will do this internally. Others will pay someone to do it for them. Companies can compete on the quality of those services while using a record their customers can inspect independently.

The rules can improve. The software can improve. The point is to make the person’s authorization and responsibility easier to identify without making ordinary work harder to do.
